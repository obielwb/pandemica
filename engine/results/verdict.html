The idea holds up for deaths; the paper's number does not. Pinning the virus to its known contagiousness and fitting only how strongly Campinas followed the measures, using nothing after 7 October 2020, the model lands at 6,188 deaths for 2020–2022 against 5,360 real ones: 15% high, where the TypeScript run was 45% high. From mid-2021 on, the simulated death curve stays close to the real one.

Counting every infection as a case, as the paper did, cannot work. Fitting that setup to 2020 cases forces an ancestral strain about 2.4 times more contagious than the literature (R0 ≈ 6.7). It still produces only a ninth of the deaths seen by October 2020, then 12.5 million "cases" by 2023. Campinas had roughly seven infections for every reported case.

Cases are where the model misses. It reports 657,300 cases against 209,500 because the reporting rate fitted on 2020 (14.6% of symptomatic infections) is held fixed, while real testing collapsed in the Omicron era. The 2020 wave also arrives three months late, so deaths overshoot at the end of 2020 before the curves meet again in 2021.

The scenario lever works. With the same calibrated model, removing lockdown, masks and school closures adds about 4,000 deaths, and removing vaccines adds almost 10,000. That is the use the paper promised health departments, and the engine now runs it in about two minutes per scenario.
